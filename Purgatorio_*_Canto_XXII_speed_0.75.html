Purgatorio * Canto XXII

As Dante, Virgil, and Statius leave the terrace of Gluttony, an angel erases the fifth "P" from Dante's brow and recites a beatitude. The three poets then begin their ascent to the sixth terrace. On the way, Virgil asks Statius how he, a man of such wisdom, could have been guilty of avarice. Statius clarifies that his sin was not avarice but its opposite, prodigality (wastefulness). He explains that he learned the virtue of moderation from reading a line in Virgil's *Aeneid*. Statius further reveals that Virgil's Fourth Eclogue inspired his secret conversion to Christianity. His fear of professing his faith openly resulted in a long penance on the terrace of Sloth. Virgil then asks about the fate of other classical poets, and Statius confirms they reside in Limbo. The group arrives on the sixth terrace, that of Lust, where they encounter a mysterious tree. A voice from within its leaves cries out examples of temperance.

Purgatorio • Canto XXII

Già era l’angel dietro a noi rimaso,
l’angel che n’avea vòlti al sesto giro,
avendomi dal viso un colpo raso;

The angel, who had turned us toward the sixth circle, had already remained behind us, having brushed a blow from my face.

e quei c’hanno a giustizia lor disiro
detto n’avea beati, e le sue voci
con ‘sitiunt’, sanz’ altro, ciò forniro.

And those who have justice in their desire, he had said, are blessed, and his words, with “sitiunt,” completed the task, nothing more.

E io più lieve che per l’altre foci
m’andava, sì che sanz’ alcun labore
seguiva in sù li spiriti veloci;

And I, lighter than I had been at the other mouths, moved onward, so that without any effort I followed the swift spirits upwards.

quando Virgilio incominciò: «Amore,
acceso di virtù, sempre altro accese,
pur che la fiamma sua paresse fore;

when Virgil began: "Love, ignited by virtue, always ignited another, so long as its flame was visible."

onde da l’ora che tra noi discese
nel limbo de lo ’nferno Giovenale,
che la tua affezion mi fé palese,

since the hour Juvenal descended into the limbo of hell among us, making your affection clear to me.

mia benvoglienza inverso te fu quale
più strinse mai di non vista persona,
sì ch’or mi parran corte queste scale.

My goodwill toward you was as strong as any unseen person ever felt, so that these stairs now seem short to me.

Ma dimmi, e come amico mi perdona
se troppa sicurtà m’allarga il freno,
e come amico omai meco ragiona:

But tell me, and as a friend, forgive me if too much certainty loosens my rein, and as a friend, now reason with me:

come poté trovar dentro al tuo seno
loco avarizia, tra cotanto senno
di quanto per tua cura fosti pieno?».

"How could avarice find a place within your heart, given the wisdom you possessed and the care you showed for others?"

Queste parole Stazio mover fenno
un poco a riso pria; poscia rispuose:
«Ogne tuo dir d’amor m’è caro cenno.

These words first brought a slight smile to Statius's face; then he replied: "Every word you speak of love is a cherished sign to me."

Veramente più volte appaion cose
che danno a dubitar falsa matera
per le vere ragion che son nascose.

Truly, often things appear that give rise to doubt, to false substance, because of the hidden, genuine reasons.

La tua dimanda tuo creder m’avvera
esser ch’i’ fossi avaro in l’altra vita,
forse per quella cerchia dov’ io era.

Your question confirms my beliefthat I was a miser in my former life,perhaps because of the sphere where I was.

Or sappi ch’avarizia fu partita
troppo da me, e questa dismisura
migliaia di lunari hanno punita.

And know that avarice was far removed from me, and this excess has punished me for thousands of lunar cycles.

E se non fosse ch’io drizzai mia cura,
quand’ io intesi là dove tu chiame,
crucciato quasi a l’umana natura:

And if I hadn't turned my attention, when I heard where you were calling from, almost angry at human nature:

‘Per che non reggi tu, o sacra fame
de l’oro, l’appetito de’ mortali?’,
voltando sentirei le giostre grame.

'Why don't you rule, O sacred hunger for gold, the appetite of mortals?' I would turn, hearing the wretched tournaments.

Allor m’accorsi che troppo aprir l’ali
potean le mani a spendere, e pente’mi
così di quel come de li altri mali.

Then I realized that my hands had spread their wings too wide, and were able to spend too much, and I repented of that, as of the other evils.

Quanti risurgeran coi crini scemi
per ignoranza, che di questa pecca
toglie ’l penter vivendo e ne li stremi!

How many will rise again with thinning hair,due to ignorance, which, while they live, preventsremorse, and does so even on their deathbeds!

E sappie che la colpa che rimbecca
per dritta opposizione alcun peccato,
con esso insieme qui suo verde secca;

And know that the sin that directly opposes another sin, with that sin, here, dries up its green.

però, s’io son tra quella gente stato
che piange l’avarizia, per purgarmi,
per lo contrario suo m’è incontrato».

But, if I have been among those peoplewho weep for avarice, to purge myself,I have encountered it through its opposite.

«Or quando tu cantasti le crude armi
de la doppia trestizia di Giocasta»,
disse ’l cantor de’ buccolici carmi,

"Or when you sang the cruel arms of Jocasta's double grief," the singer of bucolic poems said,

«per quello che Clïò teco lì tasta,
non par che ti facesse ancor fedele
la fede, sanza qual ben far non basta.

"For what Clio touches with you there, it does not seem that faith, without which doing good is not enough, yet made you faithful."

Se così è, qual sole o quai candele
ti stenebraron sì, che tu drizzasti
poscia di retro al pescator le vele?».

If that's the case, what sun or candles dispelled your shadows, that you then straightened your sails behind the fisherman?

Ed elli a lui: «Tu prima m’invïasti
verso Parnaso a ber ne le sue grotte,
e prima appresso Dio m’alluminasti.

And he to him: "You first sent metoward Parnassus to drink in its grottoes,and you first illuminated me in God's presence."

Facesti come quei che va di notte,
che porta il lume dietro e sé non giova,
ma dopo sé fa le persone dotte,

You acted like someone walking at night, who carries a light behind them, of no use to themselves, but illuminating the path for others.

quando dicesti: ‘Secol si rinova;
torna giustizia e primo tempo umano,
e progenïe scende da ciel nova’.

when you said: 'The age renews itself; justice returns, and the first human era, and a new progeny descends from heaven'.

Per te poeta fui, per te cristiano:
ma perché veggi mei ciò ch’io disegno,
a colorare stenderò la mano.

Through you I was a poet, through you a Christian: but so that you may better see what I depict, I will extend my hand to color it.

Già era ’l mondo tutto quanto pregno
de la vera credenza, seminata
per li messaggi de l’etterno regno;

Already the entire world was heavy with the true faith, sown by the messengers of the eternal kingdom;

e la parola tua sopra toccata
si consonava a’ nuovi predicanti;
ond’ io a visitarli presi usata.

and your word, once touched upon,harmonized with the new preachers;therefore, I took to visiting them.

Vennermi poi parendo tanto santi,
che, quando Domizian li perseguette,
sanza mio lagrimar non fur lor pianti;

Then, they seemed so holy that, when Domitian persecuted them, I could not help but weep for them.

e mentre che di là per me si stette,
io li sovvenni, e i lor dritti costumi
fer dispregiare a me tutte altre sette.

And while I stayed among them, I gave them aid, and their righteous customs made all other sects seem despicable to me.

E pria ch’io conducessi i Greci a’ fiumi
di Tebe poetando, ebb’ io battesmo;
ma per paura chiuso cristian fu’mi,

And before I led the Greeks to the rivers of Thebes, writing in verse, I received baptism; but, out of fear, I remained a Christian in secret.

lungamente mostrando paganesmo;
e questa tepidezza il quarto cerchio
cerchiar mi fé più che ’l quarto centesmo.

for a long time feigning paganism; and this lukewarmness made the fourth circle circle me more than four hundred times.

Tu dunque, che levato hai il coperchio
che m’ascondeva quanto bene io dico,
mentre che del salire avem soverchio,

You then, who have lifted the lidthat concealed from me the good I speak of,while we have leisure enough for the ascent,

dimmi dov’ è Terrenzio nostro antico,
Cecilio e Plauto e Varro, se lo sai:
dimmi se son dannati, e in qual vico».

Tell me where Terentius, our ancient, is,Cecilius, Plautus, and Varro, if you know:Tell me if they are damned, and in what precinct.”

«Costoro e Persio e io e altri assai»,
rispuose il duca mio, «siam con quel Greco
che le Muse lattar più ch’altri mai,

"These, and Persius, and I, and many others,"my guide replied, "are with that Greekwho the Muses nursed more than any other,

nel primo cinghio del carcere cieco;
spesse fïate ragioniam del monte
che sempre ha le nutrice nostre seco.

in the first circle of the blind prison;often we speak of the mountainthat always has our nurse with it.

Euripide v’è nosco e Antifonte,
Simonide, Agatone e altri piùe
Greci che già di lauro ornar la fronte.

Euripides is with us, and Antiphon, Simonides, Agathon, and many other Greeks whose brows were once adorned with laurel.

Quivi si veggion de le genti tue
Antigone, Deïfile e Argia,
e Ismene sì trista come fue.

Here one sees those of your people:Antigone, Deiphile, and Argia,and Ismene, as sorrowful as she was.

Védeisi quella che mostrò Langia;
èvvi la figlia di Tiresia, e Teti,
e con le suore sue Deïdamia».

Behold Langia, the one who revealed herself; there is Tiresias' daughter, and Thetis, and with her sisters, Deidamia.”

Tacevansi ambedue già li poeti,
di novo attenti a riguardar dintorno,
liberi da saliri e da pareti;

The two poets had already fallen silent, newly attentive to look around, free of the climb and of the walls.

e già le quattro ancelle eran del giorno
rimase a dietro, e la quinta era al temo,
drizzando pur in sù l’ardente corno,

And the four handmaidens of the day had already fallen behind, and the fifth was at the helm, still raising her fiery horn upward.

quando il mio duca: «Io credo ch’a lo stremo
le destre spalle volger ne convegna,
girando il monte come far solemo».

When my guide spoke: "I think we must turn to the edge, circling the mountain as we usually do."

Così l’usanza fu lì nostra insegna,
e prendemmo la via con men sospetto
per l’assentir di quell’ anima degna.

Thus, custom became our banner there, and we took the road with less suspicion, trusting the assent of that worthy soul.

Elli givan dinanzi, e io soletto
di retro, e ascoltava i lor sermoni,
ch’a poetar mi davano intelletto.

They went on ahead, and I alonebehind, listening to their discourse,which gave me the understanding to write.

Ma tosto ruppe le dolci ragioni
un alber che trovammo in mezza strada,
con pomi a odorar soavi e buoni;

But soon, a tree we found in the middle of the path broke their sweet talk, with fruit that smelled sweet and good.

e come abete in alto si digrada
di ramo in ramo, così quello in giuso,
cred’ io, perché persona sù non vada.

And as a fir tree tapers at its peak,from branch to branch, so does that one descend,I think, to keep a person from the climb.

Dal lato onde ’l cammin nostro era chiuso,
cadea de l’alta roccia un liquor chiaro
e si spandeva per le foglie suso.

On the side where our path was blocked, a clear liquid cascaded down from the high rock and spread across the leaves above.

Li due poeti a l’alber s’appressaro;
e una voce per entro le fronde
gridò: «Di questo cibo avrete caro».

The two poets drew near the tree; and a voice within the foliage cried out: "You will crave this food."

Poi disse: «Più pensava Maria onde
fosser le nozze orrevoli e intere,
ch’a la sua bocca, ch’or per voi risponde.

Then she said, "The more Mary considered wherethese worthy, holy nuptials would occur,the more she spoke of them to you now."

E le Romane antiche, per lor bere,
contente furon d’acqua; e Danïello
dispregiò cibo e acquistò savere.

And the ancient Romans, for their thirst,were content with water; and Danieldisdained food and gained wisdom.

Lo secol primo, quant’ oro fu bello,
fé savorose con fame le ghiande,
e nettare con sete ogne ruscello.

The first age, how beautiful its gold,made acorns savory through hunger,and every stream a nectar with thirst.

Mele e locuste furon le vivande
che nodriro il Batista nel diserto;
per ch’elli è glorïoso e tanto grande

Apples and locusts were the farethat nourished the Baptist in the desert;for which he is glorious and so grand.

quanto per lo Vangelio v’è aperto».

as the Gospel reveals to you."
