Inferno * Canto XIV

Dante and Virgil enter the Third Ring of the Seventh Circle, a barren plain of burning sand where flakes of fire rain down upon naked souls. They observe three distinct groups of the tormented: one group lies supine on the ground, another is huddled and crouching, and a third runs in endless circles. Dante's attention is caught by a large, defiant figure lying on the sand who seems to scorn the fiery torment. When asked, the shade identifies himself as Capaneus, one of the seven kings who besieged Thebes, and boasts that he remains as contemptuous in Hell as he was in life. Virgil sternly rebukes Capaneus, telling him that his own unquenched rage is his true punishment. Virgil then leads Dante along the edge of the burning plain, walking beside a small red stream that flows across the sand without being vaporized. As they proceed, Virgil explains that this stream, along with all the rivers of Hell, originates from the tears of a great statue of an Old Man located inside a mountain on the island of Crete.

Poi che la carità del natio loco

The sorrowful wood is a garland around us, like the dismal ditch to it. Here, we brought our steps to a halt, skirting the edge.

The expanse was a dry and dense sand, made in no other fashion than that which was already suppressed by Cato's feet.

"In the middle of the sea sits a ravaged land,"he said then, "which is called Crete,under whose king the world was once chaste."

There is a mountain that was once joyfulwith water and foliage, called Ida;now it is deserted, a forbidden thing.

Rhea had already chosen it as a sure cradle for her son, and to hide him better, when he cried, she had him cry out there.

Inside the mountain stands a great old man,who has turned his back toward Damiettaand gazes at Rome as if it were his mirror.
